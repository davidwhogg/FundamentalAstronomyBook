\chapter{Preface}
Almost everything we know about the cosmos---planets, stars, galaxies, large-scale structure, the Universe as a whole---has been inferred from the directions and wavelengths (or frequencies, or energies) of photons entering telescope apertures on or near the Earth.
Or in more classical terms, everything we know has been inferred from statistics of the specific intensity of radiation---energy per time per solid angle per area per frequency---as a function of angle and frequency, again at the entrance aperture of an Earth-proximal telescope.
Of course we have some information from gravitational waves, from neutrinos, from cosmic rays, and (in the Solar System) occasional launched probes and landers.
But the vast majority of what we know comes from electromagnetic radiation hitting the detectors in the focal planes of telescopes.

Astronomical evidence is (almost, with a few exceptions \cite{labgravity}) the only evidence we have ever had for the law of gravity and everything taking place in the gravitational sector.
Kepler's laws, Newtonian gravity, general relativity, the dark energy (or cosmological constant), the existence of dark matter, the expansion history, the growth of cosmic large-scale structure, and the existence of dark energy were all discovered, refined, and informed almost entirely with astronomical observations using photons.
Gravitational radiation has complicated this story, but only (so far) in the late stages of compact-object mergers, and even that has (so far) only confirmed the gravitational theory that we built entirely from electromagnetic observations with telescopes.

This book is about the measurement of distant objects with telescopes.
The question is:
What is it, precisely and rigorously, that we can know about the Universe, given only observations with telescopes?
Of course we won't \emph{only} have observations;
we will also have knowledge of basic physics, and especially the relativities (special and general).
We won't have any gravitational waves or neutrinos or other massless (or effectively massless) information carriers.
This is a book about traditional electromagnetic-messenger astrophysics.
That is, what does the study of light, impinging on an observatory, reveal about the cosmos?

The target audience here is comprised of physicists who want to understand the Universe and its contents, physically.
You, reader, want to measure something and you need astronomical techniques to do so.
Or, perhaps, you want to understand how something incredibly remote, like a black hole or the dark matter, could be well understood and confidently known.
This book will help you understand what has been done, what is possible, and how we will do it better in the future.

My expectation is that you know and understand basic special relativity, and the rudiments of quantum mechanics (because, for example, light is both a particle and a wave).
Basic undergraduate physics fully prepares the reader to read this book.

\chapter{What does a telescope do?}
When we first learn geometric optics in school, we learn how a telescope works.
My version of the standard diagram is in \figref{fig:telescope}.
The most important things about this \emph{highly idealized} diagram are the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item Parallel rays entering the telescope aperture come to a focus at a single point on the focal plane.
    \item Parallel lines coming from two distinct directions come to foci at two distinct points on the focal plane.
\end{itemize}
That is, the telescope maps incoming-photon \emph{angles} to focal-plane \emph{positions}.
This standard diagram (\figref{fig:telescope}) does not capture several other important points about the (idealized) telescope.
For example, it does not give any sense of the following:
\begin{itemize}
    \item The ideal telescope, which never absorbs any photons prior to their arrival at the focal plane, conserves photon phase-space density, or specific intensity, or surface brightness. This is the subject of \chapref{chap:intensity}.
    \item There are critical diffraction effects that limit the angular resolution of the image and create other telescope-aperture effects in the focal plane. This is the subject of \chapref{chap:physical}.
\end{itemize}
The first of these points relates to unitarity in electromagnetism, and will end up giving us a definition of the concept of \emph{tranparency}.
We'll expand a bit on the latter of these points right now.

You can think of a telescope as a classical device or as a quantum device, and we might switch back and forth.
If we think of the telescope as a quantum particle detector, then the incoming light can be seen as a phase-space density of photons, which is a function of 3-vector momenta $\tv{p}$.
The photon momentum $\tv{p}$ in vacuum has the property $|\tv{p}|=E/c$, where $c$ is the speed of light, $E=h\,\nu$ is the photon energy, $h$ is Planck's constant, $\nu=c/\lambda$ is the photon frequency, and $\lambda$ is the photon wavelength.
